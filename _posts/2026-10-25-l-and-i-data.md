*This blogpost is based on the amazing book by Funder (1996).*

Now that we've gone through the introduction, it is important to talk about how we actually do research in psychology. I'm not sure if anyone has ever gotten excited over research methods, but I promise it is both interesting and particularly useful to understand how research in personality psychology is done.

If we use the previously mentioned definition of personality (the patterns of thought, behaviour and emotion), we come across a big problem. These qualities are incredibly complex. They change based on the situation and are almost always ambiguous and unclear. Let's say you are given a self-assessment test with questions about your personality. You think of yourself as a very patient person and all the questions about patience you mark as 10/10. Then, your friends are given the same questionnaire and they must answer as if they were describing you.

It turns out they think of you as someone who is actually very impatient! Who is right? Do they know you better than you do? Maybe? Does their perception of you even matter?

Truthfully, they both matter. Just as a detective collects many clues in a crime scene, a fingerprint there, a burned letter there, a blood sample there, so does a personality psychologist. What you think of yourself, what others think of you, your actual life outcomes are all important clues. And they are all what we call "data".

In personality psychology, we have four types of data. Four kinds of clues. They are: **L**ife, **I**nformant, **S**elf-assessment and **T**est. These form the acronym LIST. I will go through L and I data in this post, while leaving S and T data for another post. You will see that the way that we gather, interpret and analyze data determines all the conclusions that we are able to draw. As a detective, if you cannot find any clues or only irrelevant clues, you cannot solve the case. This is the same for personality psychology.

To bring this point home, I will describe the advantages and disadvantages for each type of data to provide a better picture of why measuring personality and other psychological factors is incredibly challenging.

## Life outcomes: L data

Have you ever been hospitalized? Are you married? Did you finish university? Does your family have a history of cancer? Perhaps the simplest, but most objectively informative data is L data. They are questions about your life that are concrete, reliable and easily verifiable. This data can be easily obtained from your medical record, governmental records, or just by asking you directly. It is usually the first thing that psychologists and doctors will ask you. This kind of data has two advantages and one disadvantage.

### Intrinsic importance

What is the goal of a personality psychologist? Or a psychologist in general? It is mainly to predict and even influence someone's behaviour, their success in school, relationships and health. Let's say you go to a psychologist because you have difficulty trusting in relationships. After years of work you are now happily married and satisfied. Your life outcome (marriage) has been influenced by the psychologist. It's a very clear, objectifiable outcome. These life outcomes are L data and they tell us a lot.

### Psychological relevance

L data is strongly affected by your personality. If you are very friendly and extraverted, you might do better in sales than in sectors where you work alone. If you're more quiet and introverted, software engineering might look more attractive. On the other hand, the personality of some individuals makes them more likely to engage in criminal behaviour. So L data is not only affected by your personality but it also informs us of your personality.

### Saying too much

Now, after all this you might think that L data is amazing. And it is! L data is incredibly useful, but it sometimes tries to say too much. Think of someone losing their job. Without any context you might assume it is because they are lazy, but it might be that the business they work at went bankrupt. A friend of mine was struggling to finish university due to undiagnosed medical conditions, while a lot of people assumed it came down to laziness and boredom. Many of these situations that provide us with L data actually say nothing about personality factors.

Therefore, it is incredibly difficult to predict L data from your personality and any researcher that is able to do it, even by a small bit, should be praised.

## Just ask somebody: I data

People often say to me that I am insightful. I myself think I am fairly average in insight. I like jokes that sometimes go too far and I like to observe how people act and try to understand the way they feel. But does it truly matter what I think? If everybody thinks that I am insightful, am I actually insightful?

This is I (informant) data, just ask what others think of you. Parents, friends, teachers have a lot of things to say, some of which may end up being useful and right! This data comes with four advantages and three disadvantages.

### Large amount of information

If your parents say that you are generally shy, they have based this on hundreds or thousands or tens of thousands of situations where they have seen you acting shy. If your partner says that you are often grumpy, they must have seen you hundreds of times coming back from work and being annoyed. These conclusions of you being shy and grumpy come from an incredible amount of observation, which cannot be underestimated.

### Real-world basis

I data is important because it comes from actual observations in the real world, what we call ecological validity. So instead of some fake test or weird experiment, it is based on things that you can actually see, hear and feel in real life, like being shy at work. This data therefore is more relevant to those aspects of personality that affect behaviour and outcomes in real life.

### Common sense

That's right, common sense! Remember the example from [blogpost one]({% post_url 2026-10-11-Introduction-to-personality-psychology %}), where you and I see your boss being weirdly angry and we walk away with different conclusions. I think he's a rude person. You think something went wrong in his personal life that day. You understand that his reaction on that day was weird for him and you do not consider him to be an angry person. But why? How angry would your boss have to get for it not to be common sense anymore? How shy does someone need to act for us to consider them shy?

There are too many situations and too many variables. So any source of I data with ordinary common sense will take into account these kinds of context.

We don't have a rules-based system in psychology for this yet. It is not as difficult for a human judge as intuitions provided by common sense makes this happen naturally.

### Causal force

This advantage is quite different from the other three. I data, in a sense, is a reflection of the social world of you and represents the opinions of people you interact with. And these opinions influence you as well even if you don't know it. If someone believes you to be very extraverted and friendly, they might invite you to more parties, even if you are a very introverted person in reality. Likewise, if someone believes you to be a good planner, they might ask you to be the planner of the next group trip, regardless whether you actually are a good planner. There is also some interesting evidence that you start to become what people expect you to be, which is called the expectancy effect (more on this later!).

### Limited amount of information

Wait, didn't you just tell me that I data gives a lot of information? How is this a disadvantage? Well, if you think about it, I data gives a lot of certain types of information. Your parents know how you act in a family gathering and friends know how you act in a college party. But do your parents know your favourite alcohol is tequila? And do your friends know that you find it very hard to talk to your uncles? Most likely not. Our life is made up of these small compartments, if you will. Work, school, home, gym. And people we see there know a lot about our behaviour in those places, but basically nothing outside them. You are a different person in different environments. Some things are also very private. You might only ever cry when you're at home with your partner, or when holding your dog. You might actually be a very anxious person but have learned to keep it all inside. I data is quite useless when it comes to understanding the workings of thoughts, emotions and experience of the inner mind.

### Bias & error

You can also just be biased. Think once again of the angry boss example, we ended up with different conclusions from the same situation. I did not have the information that your boss is generally very friendly, so I created an idea that is biased. Another famous example is love. You probably know a friend that got into a relationship with someone you think is not good for them. But your friend can only talk about how great and lovely and amazing they are. They might ignore their partner's flaws because of love. Given any tests about them, they would say that they are patient, caring, extraverted and so on. Regardless whether you or they are correct, it is difficult to separate bias from I data.

You can also just be wrong. People providing I data might forget or they might not mention important details. They might focus on only very specific things. For example, I might remember that time you got drunk and got into a fight rather than all the times that you were sober. You might be an incredibly calm and collected person for the rest of your life, but that memory could be stuck in my head and influence the way I look at you.

We now have two very important sources of data: L data, which encompasses life facts and outcomes, and I data, which is what others think of you. In the next blogpost, we will take a look at S and T data.
