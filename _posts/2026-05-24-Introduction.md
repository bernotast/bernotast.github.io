# Welcome
This blogpost is based on the amazing book by Funder (1996) 

What do you think psychology is about? It is what people assume that psychology is about. “Can I change my teenager’s behaviour so they’re not so difficult all the time?” and “Why is my boss always so grumpy in meetings?” are questions that could genuinely be posed to personality psychologists and they might offer up valid answers. We pose these questions to ourselves and others all the time in our daily lives. It is simply a part of human living and therefore makes personality psychology incredibly fun and allows it to be used both for theory (e.g Why do people become less anxious when they get older?) and practice (e.g. How can I make my boss like me?). Importantly, it also makes it relevant. An experiment about a pigeon performing tricks for food might not interest you as much as an experiment about trying to change people’s moods. 

But what is personality? Personality refers to the patterns of behaviours, thoughts, emotions and any mechanisms behind those patterns (Funder, 1996). Quite a difficult definition, but let’s break it down why it is constructed that way. The key word here is patterns. For it to count as personality (trait) it must be consistent and stable. An example for consistency could be: if you are talkative at home, you are more likely to be talkative at work. If you are very talkative at home, but do not talk at all at work, there might be something wrong with your work! Or at least we would not count it as a personality trait. For stability, you should be about as talkative at 25 years old as 45 years old. Obviously you change as a person but overall your personality should be fairly stable, otherwise it would not be personality but rather a mood. 

When we talk about personality psychology we try to explain the whole individual. That is a huge undertaking. How can you explain something as complex as a person?  We come across thousands of situations on a daily basis where we respond differently depending on our mood, health, relationship with others and more factors that would take ages to list out. Trying to explain all thoughts, behaviour, emotions and what is behind them is incredibly difficult, if not impossible. So we as psychologists (But also regular people!) must limit ourselves to certain kinds of information. Certain kinds of observations, certain kinds of patterns and certain ways of thinking about these patterns. 

There are different approaches which have been used throughout the years with each one having its strengths and weaknesses. In this blogpost, I will list out all of them and will return to them in a later blogpost. Let me begin with an example of patterns.


Suppose your boss comes into the office one day and is very angry, which is unusual for him. He is usually a very relaxed person who rarely loses his cool, but today he loses his temper quickly and is clearly nervous and irritable. You get into a small argument with him about something completely unimportant. Then I come into the office for a job interview with your boss. During the interview he gets angry and is overall very unpleasant. I come out of the office thinking that he is very rude and a person I would not want to work with. The next day he was back to his usual self. It turns out he was having a very painful migraine and painkillers did not help.

Let’s think of what conclusions we would draw from this. I assume that this is how he usually is, angry and nervous.  But you, as someone who has known him for a longer time, would say that the situation was exceptional and draw an entirely different conclusion.  You know that he was having a very bad migraine and he is usually nice and relaxed. Would this one bad day say something about his actual personality? Probably not.
In this case, your observation would be much more insightful than mine, but you and I would draw different conclusions from exactly the same behaviour and situation. 

This is why we need approaches, or ways of thinking about what we see. 
 The 5 main approaches are:

Trait 
Biological
Psychoanalytic
Phenomenological
Behaviouristic

The trait approach focuses on how personality traits (Key word on traits) can be measured and conceptualized. For example, what is anger? Sadness? If someone is angry once a month, is that a personality trait? This is the most popular approach with many tests, such as the big five and Briggs - Myers (an awful test by the way) using it. 
The biological asks whether biology can be used to explain personality. Combining anatomy (How are living beings built), physiology (How do living beings work), inheritance (How do living beings pass on their genes) and evolutionary theory. For instance, if your parents are aggressive, are you also more likely to be aggressive? That is, is personality heritable?
The psychoanalytic approach is associated with Freud, one of the most influential and famous psychologists. It focuses on how the workings of the unconscious mind influences the conscious and how internal conflicts arise and are resolved. For instance, is there something deep in your mind that influences the way you choose your romantic partners? 
The phenomenological (What a mouthful) are a kind of cross of psychology and philosophy,  concerned with our experience of the world and free will and the consequences of having that free will. It is difficult to think of a clear example without going into philosophy, but think about why people in similar circumstances develop wildly different beliefs. If two individuals had a horrible childhood, they could still turn out very differently. The phenomenological approach tries to explain this difference.
Finally, behaviourists see themselves as full experimental scientists of personality psychology. The same way as sodium explodes when it comes to contact with water, the behaviourists believe that they can find consistent techniques to change someone’s behaviour. For example, if you reward someone with an ice cream if they complete a task, they will be more likely to do the task again. 

Looking back at the list you  might rightfully say that these approaches look quite disconnected. What does the unconscious mind have in common with an ice cream reward? The truth is that these approaches do not compete between themselves. They all try to handle different questions about human psychology independently. 

As an employer, you want to hire someone that is very dutiful and organized. A trait approach would be most useful here. If a religious politician is caught soliciting prostitution, questions might be raised about the unconscious workings of the mind. If a parent is concerned about their children’s behaviour, a behaviouristic approach may be useful here. 
All of these approaches have clear strengths but each strength can also be a weakness. The behaviouristic approach does not much care for unconscious processes of the mind; behaviour is all that matters. But in doing so it gives up trying to have an explanation for this behaviour, which makes it difficult to apply.  The psychoanalytic approach is famous for its pleasant and great theory about the unconscious mind, but is infamous for actually being unable to change behaviour effectively.

This is why personality psychology can seem as a subject that is impossible to research. It is either too narrow or too broad. The goal of a personality psychologist (and anyone interested in the topic) is to try to maximize the strengths of these approaches and minimize the weaknesses. This is why the first few blog posts will focus a lot on research methods. Do not worry, they will be fun and incredibly useful for understanding how personality science is actually carried out.
